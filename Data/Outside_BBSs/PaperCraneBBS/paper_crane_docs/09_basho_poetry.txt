TITLE: The Old Master - Poetry of Matsuo Bashō

CULTURAL RECOVERY DOCUMENT
Compiled by: Paper Crane Network Archivists
Source: Pre-integration literary archives, temple collections, oral tradition
Classification: FORBIDDEN HERITAGE MATERIAL

---

Matsuo Bashō (1644-1694) is considered the greatest master of haiku, the art of capturing a moment of existence in seventeen syllables. He wandered the old roads of Japan, writing poems that transformed simple observations into doorways to deeper truth.

His work was not banned outright by the Linguistic Normalisation Directive. Instead, it was simply... removed. Dropped from school curricula. Allowed to go out of print. Left untranslated. The authorities understood that a poem in a language no one can read is already silent.

We refuse that silence.

Below are selected poems with original text, romanisation, and meaning. These are not translations - translations flatten. These are attempts to carry the weight across.

---

古池や蛙飛び込む水の音

FURU IKE YA / KAWAZU TOBIKOMU / MIZU NO OTO

"Old pond / a frog jumps in / sound of water"

The most famous haiku ever written. An ancient, still pond. A single frog. The splash. That is all. But within that splash is everything - the breaking of silence, the intersection of stillness and motion, the way a single small act ripples outward through time. Bashō wrote this in 1686. The frog is still jumping. The water is still sounding. Some moments never stop happening.

---

夏草や兵どもが夢の跡

NATSUKUSA YA / TSUWAMONO-DOMO GA / YUME NO ATO

"Summer grasses / all that remains / of warriors' dreams"

Written at Hiraizumi, where the Fujiwara clan once built a golden civilization and where warriors fought and died for glory. By Bashō's time, nothing remained but grass. The warriors dreamed of eternal fame. The grass does not remember their names. This poem asks: what survives? Not power. Not conquest. Only the quiet things that keep growing after the noise stops.

---

閑さや岩にしみ入る蝉の声

SHIZUKASA YA / IWA NI SHIMIIRU / SEMI NO KOE

"Such stillness / seeping into the rocks / the cicada's cry"

A contradiction that is not a contradiction. The cicadas are screaming - anyone who has heard a Japanese summer knows the sound is deafening. And yet Bashō hears stillness. The cry is so constant, so total, that it becomes silence. It seeps into the rocks themselves. Sometimes noise is so complete it circles back to quiet. Sometimes a culture so thoroughly erased becomes, paradoxically, impossible to ignore.

---

荒海や佐渡によこたふ天河

ARAUMI YA / SADO NI YOKOTAU / AMANOGAWA

"Rough sea / stretching toward Sado / the Milky Way"

Sado Island was a place of exile, where political prisoners and poets were sent to disappear. Bashō stands on the mainland shore, looking across violent waters toward that island of the banished. Above, the Milky Way stretches in the same direction - as if even the stars are being exiled. The scale shifts from human to cosmic. Our small sufferings are written in the sky.

---

旅に病んで夢は枯野をかけ廻る

TABI NI YANDE / YUME WA KARENO WO / KAKEMEGURU

"Sick on my journey / my dreams wander / over withered fields"

Bashō's death poem, written in 1694 as illness took him. His body has stopped, but his dreams keep moving across the winter landscape. There is no self-pity here, only observation: this is what happens. The body fails. The mind keeps wandering. The fields are withered but the dreams still run. He died four days after writing this. The dreams are still running.

---

枯枝に烏のとまりけり秋の暮

KAREEDA NI / KARASU NO TOMARIKERI / AKI NO KURE

"On a bare branch / a crow has settled / autumn evening"

Black bird. Bare branch. Fading light. The poem is almost colourless - just shapes against a dimming sky. There is no meaning to extract here, no lesson. Only the image itself, which lands in the mind and refuses to leave. This is what haiku does at its best: it does not tell you what to think. It shows you something and then stops talking.

---

五月雨をあつめて早し最上川

SAMIDARE WO / ATSUMETE HAYASHI / MOGAMIGAWA

"Gathering the rains / of May, how swift it runs / the Mogami River"

The spring rains fall across the mountains. Each drop is small, gentle, harmless. But they gather. They collect. They become a river running so fast it is dangerous to cross. Bashō is not just describing weather. He is describing accumulation - how small things, given time, become torrents. How many raindrops make a flood? How many whispered words make a resistance?

---

初しぐれ猿も小蓑をほしげ也

HATSU SHIGURE / SARU MO KOMINO WO / HOSHIGE NARI

"First winter rain / even the monkey seems to want / a little straw coat"

Bashō sees a monkey in the cold rain and imagines its desire for warmth, for shelter, for the simple protection humans take for granted. The poem is tender and slightly funny - the monkey's dignity, its quiet suffering, its very monkey-ness caught in a moment of weather. Compassion does not require grand gestures. Sometimes it is just noticing that another creature is cold.

---

名月や池をめぐりて夜もすがら

MEIGETSU YA / IKE WO MEGURITE / YO MO SUGARA

"Harvest moon / walking around the pond / all night long"

The full autumn moon, the most celebrated moon of the year. Bashō does not sit and contemplate it. He walks. He circles the pond, watching the moon's reflection move with him, for the entire night. There is something almost obsessive here, a refusal to let the beautiful moment end. Sometimes the correct response to beauty is not stillness but restless, grateful motion.

---

行く春や鳥啼き魚の目は泪

YUKU HARU YA / TORI NAKI UO NO / ME WA NAMIDA

"Spring departing / birds cry, and in the eyes of fish / there are tears"

Written as Bashō left his home to begin his famous journey to the deep north. Everything weeps at his departure - the birds in the air, the fish in the water. Of course fish do not cry. Of course they do. The whole world participates in the grief of leaving. When we depart from a place we love, we are not the only ones who feel it.

---

この道や行く人なしに秋の暮

KONO MICHI YA / YUKU HITO NASHI NI / AKI NO KURE

"This road / no one walks it / autumn evening"

Near the end of his life, Bashō looks at a path and sees no travellers. The autumn light is fading. The road continues into darkness, and he is alone on it. There is loneliness here, but also something else - the road exists whether or not anyone walks it. The path is real even when empty. Perhaps especially when empty.

---

ARCHIVIST CLOSING NOTE:

Bashō believed that to write haiku, you must first become the thing you observe. To write about the frog, become the frog. To write about the crow, become the crow. 

This is not mysticism. It is attention so complete that the boundary between observer and observed dissolves.

In the Pacifica Isles, we have been taught to observe our own culture from outside - as tourists in our own inheritance. Bashō teaches the opposite: go inward. Become the thing. Let the boundary dissolve.

Seventeen syllables. A whole universe.
