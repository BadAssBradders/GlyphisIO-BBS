TITLE: Voices From Before - Preserved Proverbs

CULTURAL RECOVERY DOCUMENT
Compiled by: Paper Crane Network Archivists
Source: Oral recordings, estate salvage, temple ruins
Classification: FORBIDDEN HERITAGE MATERIAL

---

The proverbs below were once taught to children, spoken by elders, painted on shop walls. They carried centuries of accumulated wisdom in a handful of syllables. When the Linguistic Normalisation Directive took effect, these phrases became contraband - evidence of a language that was supposed to disappear.

We preserve them here not as museum pieces, but as living thought.

Read them. Memorise them. Pass them on.

---

七転び八起き
NANA KOROBI YA OKI
"Fall seven times, stand up eight."

Failure is not the opposite of success - it is the path toward it. This proverb teaches that resilience matters more than perfection. The person who falls seven times and rises eight has not failed seven times; they have practiced standing up. In the Pacifica Isles, where an entire culture was knocked down, this phrase takes on new weight. We have fallen. We are still rising.

---

猿も木から落ちる
SARU MO KI KARA OCHIRU
"Even monkeys fall from trees."

Even experts make mistakes. Even masters stumble. This proverb is a lesson in humility and forgiveness - for others and for yourself. The monkey lives in trees, knows every branch, and still sometimes falls. Perfection is not the standard. Continued effort is. When you see someone fail at something they should know well, remember the monkey. When you fail at something you thought you'd mastered, remember you are in good company.

---

出る杭は打たれる
DERU KUI WA UTARERU
"The nail that sticks out gets hammered down."

A warning about conformity, often misread by Westerners as an endorsement of it. The proverb observes a social reality without celebrating it. Yes, those who stand out attract attention and punishment. But the proverb does not say the nail is wrong to stick out - only that it will face the hammer. Some nails accept the hammering and stay down. Others keep rising. The occupation relied on this principle. They hammered every nail they could find. They are still hammering. We are still rising.

---

井の中の蛙大海を知らず
I NO NAKA NO KAWAZU TAIKAI WO SHIRAZU
"A frog in a well does not know the great ocean."

Limited experience creates limited understanding. The frog believes the well is the entire world because the frog has never left the well. This proverb warns against mistaking your small view for the complete picture. In the Pacifica Isles, we are all frogs now - raised in a well built by others, taught that the well is all there is. The archive exists to show you the ocean.

---

継続は力なり
KEIZOKU WA CHIKARA NARI
"Continuity is power."

Persistence defeats talent. Steady effort over time accomplishes what bursts of brilliance cannot. This proverb is not about dramatic acts of will; it is about showing up, day after day, doing the small work. A river cuts through rock not through force but through persistence. A language survives not through revolution but through whispers. Keep speaking. Keep remembering. Continuity is power.

---

花より団子
HANA YORI DANGO
"Dumplings over flowers."

Substance over style. Practicality over beauty. This proverb gently mocks those who choose the aesthetic over the useful - why admire cherry blossoms when you could eat dumplings instead? But there is a deeper reading: do not let appearances distract you from what truly nourishes. The occupation gave us American-style prosperity, neon signs, consumer goods, a beautiful surface. The dumplings - identity, heritage, memory - were taken away. Choose the dumplings.

---

七転八倒
SHICHI TEN BATTOU
"Seven falls, eight agonies."

Sometimes translated as "writhing in agony" - the experience of being knocked down over and over, twisting in pain, unable to find stable ground. Unlike "fall seven times, stand up eight," this proverb acknowledges that sometimes the falling itself is the whole experience. Sometimes you do not rise immediately. Sometimes you simply endure. This is also valid. This is also survival.

---

虎穴に入らずんば虎子を得ず
KOKETSU NI IRAZUNBA KOJI WO EZU
"If you do not enter the tiger's cave, you will not catch its cub."

Nothing ventured, nothing gained. To achieve something valuable, you must accept risk. The tiger's cave is dangerous - that is why the cub is worth having. This proverb speaks to every operator who runs a pirate broadcast, every archivist who saves a forbidden document, every person who speaks a banned word. Yes, it is dangerous. Yes, we enter anyway. The cub is worth it.

---

能ある鷹は爪を隠す
NOU ARU TAKA WA TSUME WO KAKUSU
"The hawk with talent hides its talons."

True ability does not need to announce itself. The skilled hunter does not display its weapons until the moment of use. In a surveillance state, this proverb becomes survival advice: do not show what you can do until you must. Let them underestimate you. Let them think you are harmless. When the moment comes, your talons will be ready.

---

雨降って地固まる
AME FUTTE JI KATAMARU
"After rain, the ground hardens."

Adversity strengthens foundations. Relationships tested by conflict become stronger if they survive. The earth that has been softened by rain becomes firmer once it dries. This proverb offers hope to those in the middle of the storm: the difficulty you face now is making something stronger for later. The occupation was the rain. We are the ground. We are hardening.

---

ARCHIVIST CLOSING NOTE:

These phrases survived because people refused to let them die. Grandmothers whispered them to grandchildren. Workers scratched them into break room walls. Students passed them in folded notes.

Language is not just words. Language is how a people think, how they frame the world, how they pass meaning from one generation to the next.

When they took the language, they tried to take the thinking.

They failed.

Memorise one proverb. Teach it to someone else. The chain continues.

- PAPER_CRANE
